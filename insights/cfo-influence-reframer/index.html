CFO Influence in 2027: Why the Reframer Wins

By the CFOLogic team

Idea in brief

Why influence has become the CFO's central skill as finance moves into the operating teams, how influence is built from time and trust, why reframing beats adaptability, worked examples of reframing faster data and revenue growth, and the Claim, Check, Change exercise CFOs used at recent roundtables to test whether the skills they claim show up in their work.

Why influence now. Finance is embedded in operations rather than sitting apart from them. The CFO's reach depends on whether operating leaders seek out the finance view, not on reporting lines.
What it is made of. Time and trust. Influence comes from being in the business long enough to understand it, and being right often enough to be believed. Lose either and it goes.
Reframing over adapting. Adaptability responds to the situation. Reframing changes how the situation is understood, moving people from what they ask for to what they need.
How to test it. Claim a skill, check it against a real example from the last 60 days, and decide what to change. Then ask a peer to score the example.

Ask a room of CFOs what got them the job and most will describe technical strength: the close they fixed, the model that held up in diligence, the refinancing they ran. Ask what decides whether they succeed in the job and the answer is different: CFO influence, which almost nobody was trained in.

That was the thread through the CFO Alliance roundtables in Texas in early November 2026. The finance function has moved from a separate vertical, reporting up through the CFO, to an embedded model where finance people sit inside sales, operations and product. In that model the CFO cannot direct most of the decisions that matter. They can only shape them. And as one participant put it, they have to do it without pointy elbows.

Why CFO influence matters more now

The embedded model gives finance reach and takes away control. A finance partner sitting with the operations team sees decisions earlier, but the operations leader makes them. The CFO's effect on the business now runs through other people's choices.

At the same time the job is turning over faster. CFOs in the room were frank that the hard technical skills that won the seat matter less each year, and the soft skills matter more: communication, change management, and the ability to tell the story behind the numbers. Several called them the hardest skills of all.

Influence is built from time and trust

One definition from the room is worth keeping: influence is the output of time and trust. Lose either and it collapses.

Time means being in the business, not just reporting on it. Knowing the operations leader well enough to understand what keeps them up at night. Showing up in the weekly sales meeting with a view, not a report.

Trust means being right, being consistent, and being fair when the numbers are bad news. It is also earned in small things: the forecast that held, the commitment that was kept, the problem raised early rather than at quarter end.

And perception counts. If the message does not land, the intent behind it does not matter. A correct analysis delivered badly loses to a weaker one delivered well.

Reframing beats adaptability

Adaptability is the usual answer to what a modern CFO needs. Several CFOs preferred a different word: reframer.

Adaptability is reactive. The business changes and the CFO adjusts. Reframing is owning the narrative: moving a stakeholder from what they asked for to what they actually need, and doing it in a way they accept.

Two examples from the rooms show the difference.

"We need faster data." Management asks for daily dashboards and a quicker close. The reframe is that faster data will not, by itself, produce better decisions. What limits the decisions is upstream: how orders are recorded, when costs are captured, who owns the inputs. Those changes take quarters, not weeks. The CFO who says so, and sets out the sequence, gets a better result than the one who delivers a faster dashboard over the same bad inputs.

"Revenue is up, so we are fine." Many CFOs in the room still spend real effort on the difference between revenue and cash. The reframe is to change the question the leadership team asks each month, from how much did we sell to how much did we collect and what did it cost to deliver. We cover how to present it in from data to decisions.

Claim, Check, Change

The roundtables used a short exercise to test whether the skills CFOs claim actually show up in their work. It takes ten minutes and works best in pairs.

StepThe questionWhat a good answer looks like
ClaimWhat skill are you claiming for 2027?One skill, named precisely, such as reframing, not a list
CheckWhere did you apply it in the last 60 days?A specific situation, what you did and what changed. Rate it from 1 to 5, then have a peer rate the same example
ChangeHow will you strengthen it, or build it in others?One concrete action with a date, or one person you will coach

The gap between your own score and your peer's is the useful number. A skill claimed with no recent example, or rated well by its owner and poorly by a colleague, is a skill that is not yet being deployed. CFOs in the room said they would run it with their teams as well.

What influence will not fix

It will not fix weak numbers. Influence built on a late close or a forecast that keeps moving runs out quickly. Get the foundations right first; we look at what that takes in the CFO isn't failing, the finance function around them is.

It will not make every disagreement go your way. Reframing changes how a decision is understood, not who owns it. Sometimes the right outcome is a well-understood decision you would not have made.

Frequently asked questions

Why is influence important for a CFO?

Because finance is now embedded in the operating teams rather than sitting apart, and most of the decisions that affect financial results are made by other leaders. A CFO's impact depends on whether those leaders seek out and act on the finance view.

How does a CFO build influence?

Through time and trust. Time means being in the business and understanding the operating leaders' problems. Trust comes from being right, consistent and early with bad news. Lose either and influence falls away.

What does it mean for a CFO to be a reframer?

A reframer moves stakeholders from what they ask for to what they need, and owns the narrative around a decision. Adaptability responds to change; reframing shapes how the change is understood.

What is the Claim, Check, Change exercise?

A short self-assessment. Claim one skill for the coming year, check it against a specific example from the last 60 days and rate it, have a peer rate the same example, then decide on one change to strengthen the skill or build it in others.

Are soft skills more important than technical skills for CFOs?

Technical skill is still the entry ticket, but it no longer decides success. Communication, change management and the ability to explain the numbers in the business's terms increasingly do, and CFOs describe them as the hardest skills to build.
