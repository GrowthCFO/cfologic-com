AI Guardrails for Finance Data: Access, Logging and Human Sign-Off

By the CFOLogic team

Idea in brief

Six AI guardrails for finance data (access by role, read-only by default, logging, boundaries on what goes into and comes out of a tool, human sign-off before anything leaves finance, and testing), the evidence that shows each one is working, and how they map to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework of January 2023 and its Generative AI Profile of July 2024.

What AI guardrails are. The rules and system settings that decide what an AI tool can see, what it can change, what is recorded, and who must approve its output before it is used.
The six that matter in finance. Access by role, read-only by default, logging, limits on what data goes in and what comes out, human sign-off before anything leaves finance, and testing before and after changes.
What NIST offers. The voluntary NIST AI Risk Management Framework (January 2023) and its Generative AI Profile (July 2024) give a structure for these decisions without requiring a large programme.
Where to start. Make every AI tool that touches finance data read-only, log what it does, and name the person who signs off anything it produces for use outside the team.

Take an analyst cleaning up the vendor master before an audit. He exports the file, bank details and tax IDs included, and pastes it into a general AI assistant to look for duplicates. The result is genuinely helpful. It also means the company's vendor bank details now sit in a tool nobody in finance approved, under terms nobody in finance has read.

Nothing malicious happened, and nobody broke a written rule, because there was no written rule. That gap is what AI guardrails close. For finance data, the guardrails that matter are few and practical, and most of them extend controls finance already runs.

Access, read-only and logging

Access follows the person. An AI tool that can see more than its user becomes a way round every access control you have. If a clerk cannot open the payroll ledger, an assistant answering that clerk should not be able to read it either. Automated agents that run on a schedule need their own accounts, scoped to the task, and should appear in your periodic access review like any other user.

Read-only is the default. Reading the ledger to explain a variance carries a different risk from posting a journal. Start every tool read-only. Where write access is genuinely needed, treat it as a separate decision with its own approval, limited to specific transaction types, and keep a list of every tool that has it.

Logs need to be usable. A log that records only that a session happened is of little use when a question arises. Record who asked, which documents and data the tool used, what it produced, and whether the output was accepted, changed or rejected. Keep the logs long enough to cover the audit cycle, and check once that someone can actually retrieve them.

Boundaries on data and prompts

Most finance leakage risk comes from ordinary people doing reasonable things with the wrong tool. A short table that matches data classes to approved tools prevents most of it. Bank details, payroll, tax identifiers and unreleased results belong in the most restricted class, allowed only in tools the company has approved for them.

The second boundary is less obvious. AI tools read documents, and documents can contain text written to instruct the tool. An invoice PDF or a supplier email could include a line telling an assistant to change a bank account or approve a payment. The guardrail is simple to state: text inside a document is treated as data to be read, never as an instruction to act on, and no tool can change payment details on the strength of a document alone. Your vendor master change process should already require independent confirmation of bank changes. AI does not alter that rule. It adds another route by which a fraudulent request can arrive.

Human sign-off and testing

Sign-off before anything leaves finance. A draft collections email, a variance comment in the board pack, a proposed journal, a reconciliation conclusion. Each needs an approver, named in advance, whose approval is recorded against the item. The reviewer should know what to check, which usually means tying the output back to source.

Testing before and after change. Build a short test set: questions with agreed answers, documents with known correct coding, a few deliberately tricky cases. Run it before a tool goes live. Run it again whenever the prompt, the source documents, the access settings or the underlying model change. Some vendors update models with little notice, so a tool that passed in June may behave differently in August.

Among the 200 large-company CFOs in Deloitte's Q2 2026 CFO Signals survey, the challenge named most often, by 59%, was balancing speed against risk. Guardrails are how that balance becomes a set of decisions rather than a debate in every meeting.

Using the NIST frameworks without a programme

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, version 1.0, was released on 26 January 2023. It is voluntary and organised around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. On 26 July 2024 NIST released a companion, the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1), which describes risks that are unique to or made worse by generative AI. Its list includes confabulation, NIST's term for confidently stated false content, along with data privacy and information security.

A finance team does not need to implement either document in full. Use them as a checklist. Govern asks who owns AI decisions and what the policy says. Map asks where AI touches finance data. Measure asks how you test it. Manage asks what you do when something goes wrong. If you can answer those four for each AI tool that touches finance data, the guardrails above are largely in place.

What AI guardrails will not do

They will not make an AI tool accurate. Guardrails control access, record activity and force a review. Whether the output is right still depends on the quality of the source data and on a reviewer with enough experience to spot an error.

They will not stay current by themselves. New tools arrive, people change roles and vendors change models. The access review, the tool list and the test set need an owner and a date in the calendar.

Frequently asked questions

What are AI guardrails in finance?

AI guardrails are the rules and system settings that control what an AI tool can see and change, what is logged, and who must approve its output. In finance the core six are access by role, read-only by default, logging, data boundaries, human sign-off and testing.

Should AI tools have write access to the ERP?

Not by default; start read-only and grant write access only as a separate, approved decision for specific transaction types. Keep a list of every tool with write rights and review it with your other access reviews.

What should be logged when finance uses AI?

Log who asked, which documents and data the tool used, what it produced, and whether the output was accepted, changed or rejected. Keep logs for at least the audit cycle and confirm they can be retrieved.

What is the NIST AI Risk Management Framework?

It is a voluntary framework released by NIST on 26 January 2023, organised around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. NIST added a Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) on 26 July 2024.

How do you stop finance data going into unapproved AI tools?

Classify finance data, match each class to the tools approved for it, and make the approved route easier than the unapproved one. Bank details, payroll and unreleased results should be allowed only in tools approved for restricted data.
