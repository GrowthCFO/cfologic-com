Management by Exception in Finance: Designing Work Around What Needs a Person

By the CFOLogic team

Idea in brief

How to apply management by exception in finance: defining normal with rules, setting thresholds by value and by risk, giving every exception type one owner, making age visible, escalating on a schedule, sampling what clears automatically, where AI helps with sorting and preparing decisions, and an exception board shown in a demo.

What management by exception means in finance. Rules handle the normal items, and anything outside a rule goes to a named person with a clock running on it.
How to set exception thresholds. By value for routine variances, and by risk for events that always need a person, such as a change to supplier bank details, whatever the amount.
Who should own an exception. One named person per exception type, with a defined escalation path to a team lead and then a controller if it ages.
Where AI fits. Sorting exceptions by reason and preparing the facts a person needs to decide; a person still approves anything that releases cash or changes master data.

Thursday morning, payment run day. The payables manager opens the list of invoices due and starts scrolling through six hundred lines. She is looking for perhaps a dozen: two blocked for price differences, one from a supplier whose bank details changed on Monday, a credit note that was never applied, a few with no goods receipt. The other lines are fine, and she reads them anyway, because the report has no way of telling her which ones need her.

Management by exception is the fix for that morning. The process is designed so that rules clear the normal items, and people see only the items that need judgment, each with an owner and an age. The idea is old. What has changed is how much of the sorting software can now do before anyone opens the list.

What management by exception means in finance

It has three parts. First, normal is defined in rules: the purchase order, receipt and invoice agree within tolerance; the bank line matches an open item; the accrual is within a set range of last month. Second, items that meet the rules move on without a person touching them. Third, everything else becomes an exception, routed to a named person, timed from the moment it is raised, and recorded when it is resolved.

The test of a good design is what the person sees on a normal day. If the answer is a full transaction list, the process is still built around review of everything. If it is a short queue of items, each with a reason and an age, it is built around exceptions.

Set thresholds by value and by risk. Value thresholds suit routine variances. Risk rules suit events that need a person regardless of amount: a new supplier, a changed bank account, a payment to a country you do not usually pay, a manual journal to a sensitive account.

Tune them against the exception rate. Ardent Partners puts the average invoice exception rate at 18.4% in its sponsor-underwritten payables research, which does not state a sample. If something close to one invoice in five is an exception, handling exceptions is a large part of the process, not a side task. Track your own rate by reason code each month. A rising rate for one reason usually points to an upstream problem that a threshold change will only hide.

One owner per exception type. The owner is a person, not a team. Where the cause sits outside finance, as with missing goods receipts, the owner can sit outside finance too, provided the clock and the escalation still apply.

Escalate on a schedule. Agree in advance when an aged exception moves up, and to whom. Then the payment run cut-off stops being the moment exceptions get noticed.

Where AI helps, and where it stops

AI is useful in two places. The first is sorting. Matching and classification tools can separate items that clearly match from those that nearly match and those that do not. Microsoft's Financial Reconciliation agent is one example. Its prerelease documentation (updated 27 May 2026) offers assistive and autonomous modes and places each transaction in one of three groups: "unmatched, potentially matched, and perfectly matched". The middle category is where a person's time is best spent.

The second is preparing the decision. For each exception, AI can gather what the person would otherwise look up: the purchase order history, prior price changes from that supplier, the last correspondence, a draft query to the buyer. The person decides faster because the facts are already assembled.

Where it should stop is equally clear. AI should not set or loosen its own thresholds. It should not clear exceptions that release cash or change supplier master data without a person approving. Each decision a person makes should be recorded, with what the AI suggested alongside it, so that the review trail survives an audit.

What an exception board looks like

An exception board is the screen that replaces the six-hundred-line list. It shows open exceptions grouped by status and owner, how long each has waited, and what happens next. Our P2P Command Center is a demo of one, built for a fictional company with fictional data. As designed, it reads purchase-to-pay data from SAP without writing anything back, lays invoices out by owner and by status, and prepares reminder drafts that a person reviews and sends. It is a demonstration of the pattern rather than a system in use.

Whatever tool you use, the board should answer four questions at a glance: what is waiting, why, who owns it, and for how long.

What management by exception will not fix

It will not fix poor rules. If tolerances are wrong or reason codes are vague, exceptions reach the right people faster and still cannot be resolved. Writing the rules down properly usually takes longer than building the board.

It also creates a blind spot inside "normal". Items that clear automatically are seen by nobody, and some fraud is designed to sit just under a threshold, such as an invoice split into two smaller ones. Sample a few auto-cleared items every month, and review the thresholds themselves at least once a year.

Frequently asked questions

What is management by exception in finance?

It is a way of designing finance work so that rules handle routine items and people see only the items that fall outside those rules. Each exception has a reason, a named owner, a visible age and a defined escalation path.

How should exception thresholds be set?

Use value thresholds for routine variances and risk rules for events that always need a person, such as changed supplier bank details or a new supplier. Review thresholds against the monthly exception rate by reason code.

Who should own a finance exception?

One named person per exception type, who may sit outside finance where the cause does, such as a buyer for price variances. Shared inboxes without a named owner are where exceptions age.

How does AI support management by exception?

AI sorts items into matched, nearly matched and unmatched, and gathers the facts a person needs to decide each exception. A person still approves anything that releases cash or changes master data.

What is the main risk of management by exception?

Items that clear automatically are not looked at by anyone. Regular sampling of auto-cleared items and an annual review of thresholds reduce the chance that errors or fraud sit unseen below the line.
