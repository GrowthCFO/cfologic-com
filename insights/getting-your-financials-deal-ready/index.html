Getting Your Financials Deal-Ready: What Buyers and Lenders Look For

By the CFOLogic team

Idea in brief

A seller's guide to deal-ready financials — what buyers and lenders actually test, what SBA lenders now require under SOP 50 10 8.1, the statements and schedules to prepare, the issues that slow diligence, whether to commission a sell-side quality of earnings, and a timeline from two years out to the data room.

What deal-ready means. Financial statements a buyer or lender can rely on without rebuilding them: accrual basis, reconciled, consistent from year to year, with every adjustment documented.
What buyers and lenders check. Whether earnings are real, whether they repeat, whether they will survive a change of owner, and whether they can carry the debt the deal needs.
What SBA lenders now require. For loans issued an SBA number from 1 October 2026, a first-time buyer needs 1.25x debt service coverage on historical or adjusted earnings, and deals of $3 million and above need a lender-ordered quality of earnings with a cash proof.
When to start. Ideally two years before going to market. The cleanup takes months, and buyers read trends across several years.

Most owners prepare for a sale by working on the business. Revenue, margin, customers, the team. All of that matters. What many miss is that the buyer cannot see any of it directly. They see it through the financial statements, and they will test those statements harder than any lender or tax authority has before.

A business with strong results and weak books sells for less, takes longer to sell, or does not sell at all. The good news is that books are fixable. It takes time, and the time is the part owners underestimate.

What deal-ready actually means

Deal-ready financials are statements a buyer, a lender or a reviewer can rely on without rebuilding them. In practice that means four things.

Accrual basis. Revenue recorded when earned and expenses when incurred, not when cash moves. Many small businesses keep cash-basis books for tax. Buyers value on accrual, and a cash-basis file will be converted during diligence whether you do it or they do.

Reconciled. Every bank, card, payment processor and loan account reconciled every month, with no unexplained differences carried forward.

Consistent. The same accounts, the same classifications and the same policies from year to year, so a trend means something.

Documented. Every adjustment, add-back and one-off item supported by a document someone else can check.

What buyers and lenders are actually testing

Behind every diligence request sits one of four questions.

Are the earnings real? Does revenue tie to the bank? Are expenses complete? A proof of cash answers this directly.

Will they repeat? Is revenue recurring or project-based? Are there one-off gains in the numbers, or one-off costs the seller wants added back?

Will they survive a change of owner? Does revenue depend on the seller personally, on a single customer, or on a key employee? Customer concentration is the usual focus.

Can they carry the debt? If the buyer is borrowing, the lender tests debt service coverage on the historical numbers. That test sets how much the buyer can pay.

The first question is about your books. The other three are about your business, but they are answered from your books, which is why the books matter so much.

What SBA lenders now require

Many small business acquisitions are financed with SBA 7(a) loans, so SBA rules shape what those buyers can pay. SOP 50 10 8.1 applies to loans issued an SBA loan number on or after 1 October 2026, and it changed three things a seller should know.

Coverage on history, not projections. A first-time buyer must show at least 1.25x debt service coverage, up from 1.15x, measured on the seller's historical or adjusted earnings. Post-closing projections cannot be used to reach it. Your trailing numbers, and the add-backs you can support, now set the ceiling on what an SBA-financed buyer can borrow.

Quality of earnings at $3 million and above. Acquisitions with a purchase price of $3 million or more need a quality of earnings report ordered by the lender, including a cash proof.

More buyer cash. The minimum equity injection for an initial acquisition stays at 10%, but at least half must be the buyer's own unborrowed cash. That narrows the pool of buyers who can fund a deal and puts more weight on seller financing. We cover the detail in seller financing under SOP 50 10 8.1.

Buyers who are permanent residents rather than citizens can no longer use SBA loans at all since 1 March 2026, which also changes who is in the market. See buying a business as a green card holder.

Monthly matters. Annual statements hide seasonality, one-off months and the trend inside the year, which is what buyers most want to see.

The issues that slow diligence down

These are the problems that turn a six-week diligence into a four-month one, or end it.

Cash-basis books. The conversion to accrual happens during diligence, under time pressure, and often moves earnings in ways the seller did not expect.

Unreconciled accounts. If the books do not agree to the bank, every other number is in question.

Personal and business money mixed. Personal spending through the business may be a legitimate add-back. Without a clean list, it looks like poor control, and every add-back gets challenged.

Add-backs without evidence. An add-back you cannot document will be removed, and the buyer will discount the rest. Keep the invoice, the contract or the reason for every one.

Revenue in the wrong period. Invoices dated before work is done, deposits treated as revenue, or year-end revenue pulled forward. A reviewer finds these in the cut-off testing.

Tax and payroll compliance gaps. Unfiled sales tax in states where the business has nexus, payroll tax issues, or contractors who should have been employees. These become indemnities or price reductions.

Related-party arrangements. Rent paid to the owner's property company at above or below market, or services from family members. These need to be identified and normalised.

Liabilities nobody recorded. Customer deposits, accrued bonuses, warranty obligations and deferred revenue. On cash-basis books they often do not exist until diligence finds them.

Should you commission a sell-side quality of earnings?

A sell-side quality of earnings is a review you commission before going to market. It does not replace the buyer's own diligence or the lender's report. What it does is find the problems before the buyer does, while you still have time to fix them or explain them.

It is most useful when the books have known weaknesses, when the deal is large enough that a surprise would move the price materially, or when you expect several buyers and want the process to move quickly. For SBA deals of $3 million and above, remember the lender will still order its own.

If you do not commission one, do the parts that matter most yourself: a monthly proof of cash, an add-back schedule with evidence, and a working capital history by month.

A timeline for getting ready

WhenWhat to do
Two years outMove to accrual, reconcile monthly, separate personal spending, fix the chart of accounts so years compare
Eighteen to twelve months outClose the books on a fixed date each month, start a documented add-back schedule, resolve tax and payroll compliance gaps
Twelve to six months outBuild monthly working capital history, revenue by customer, and a proof of cash; consider a sell-side quality of earnings
Six to three months outAssemble the data room, prepare the schedules above, and rehearse the explanations for every unusual month
In the processAnswer requests from one prepared source, quickly and consistently

Two years sounds long. It is the time needed to show a buyer two clean years of trend, which is what they are paying for.

Common mistakes

Fixing the books during diligence. Changes made while a buyer is watching look like the numbers were moved for the sale.

Overstating add-backs. Aggressive add-backs reduce trust in all of them. A shorter, well-documented list is worth more than a long one.

Ignoring working capital. Owners focus on price and forget that the working capital peg can move the cash they actually receive.

One person holds all the answers. If only the owner or one bookkeeper can explain the numbers, diligence slows to their availability.

What deal-ready books will not do

They will not raise the value of a weak business. They show the business as it is. If margins are thin or revenue is declining, clean books make that clear rather than hiding it.

They will not remove business risk either. A business that depends on its owner or on one customer will still be priced for that risk, however good the financials are. Clean books mean the buyer prices the real risk rather than adding a discount for uncertainty.

Frequently asked questions

What financial statements do buyers want when buying a business?

Usually monthly profit and loss statements and balance sheets for three years plus the current year to date, business tax returns for three years, bank statements for the review period, receivables and payables ageing, revenue by customer, and a schedule of add-backs with evidence.

How far in advance should I prepare my financials for a sale?

Ideally two years. Buyers value the trend over several years, and moving to accrual accounting, reconciling monthly and resolving compliance gaps takes months before the results show in the numbers.

What do lenders look for in a business acquisition?

Whether historical earnings can cover the new debt. For SBA loans issued an SBA number from 1 October 2026, a first-time buyer needs at least 1.25x debt service coverage on historical or adjusted earnings, not projections.

Do I need a quality of earnings report to sell my business?

Not always, but the buyer will usually commission one, and for SBA-financed deals of $3 million or more the lender must order one with a cash proof. A sell-side report helps you find and fix problems before buyers see them.

Should my books be on accrual or cash basis before a sale?

Accrual. Buyers and lenders value businesses on accrual earnings, and a cash-basis file will be converted during diligence. Converting early lets you see and explain the effect before a buyer does.

What slows down due diligence the most?

Unreconciled accounts, cash-basis books, personal spending mixed with business spending, add-backs without supporting evidence, revenue recorded in the wrong period, and unrecorded liabilities such as customer deposits or deferred revenue.

What is an add-back schedule?

A list of adjustments to reported earnings, such as owner expenses or genuinely one-off costs, each with the amount, the reason it will not continue under a new owner, and the document that supports it.
