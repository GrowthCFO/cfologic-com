Architect, Builder or Foreman: The Three Types of CFO and Which One You Need

By the CFOLogic team

Idea in brief

Three types of CFO — the architect who designs the finance function, the builder who stands it up, and the foreman who runs it day to day — why one person rarely covers all three, how the need changes across a company's stages and a private equity hold, and how to hire or structure around the gap.

The architect. Designs the finance function: the operating model, systems, controls and reporting a business will need at its next stage.
The builder. Stands it up: implements the systems, writes the processes, hires the team and gets the first clean closes out.
The foreman. Runs it: controls the workflow, puts the right people on the right work, trains them and keeps the function improving.
The point. One person rarely does all three well, and that is fine. Hire for the stage the business is entering, and cover the other two deliberately.

Ask a board what kind of CFO it wants and the answer is usually everything. Someone strategic enough to design the finance function for the next five years, practical enough to implement it, and steady enough to run it every month. That person exists, occasionally. More often the search produces a strong candidate in one of the three types of CFO and an assumption that they will grow into the others.

At CFO Alliance roundtables in Texas in early November 2026, finance leaders kept returning to a simpler way to describe the role: architecting the box, building the box and running the box. The three types of CFO it implies are a more honest basis for a hire than a single list of everything.

Three types of CFO

The architect. Designs the finance function. Decides what the business will need to report, control and forecast at its next stage, and what operating model, systems and team will deliver it. An architect thinks in blueprints and is often less interested in the construction.

The builder. Stands it up. Implements the ERP, writes the close calendar and the policies, hires the first controller and analysts, and gets the first clean closes out the door. Builders work in the trenches, which is also the risk: there is little bandwidth left for influence across the business.

The foreman. Runs it. Controls the workflows, places people on the right work, owns training and keeps the function improving once it exists. Several CFOs in the room argued this is the role that matters most now, as finance moves from a separate department into the operating teams it supports.

Why one person rarely covers all three

These skills pull in different directions. Design needs distance from the detail; a build needs someone deep in it. Running the function takes patience with routine and a talent for developing other people, which the other two roles reward less.

They also take different amounts of time. A build can absorb a year of the CFO's attention. While that happens, the work that earns influence with the CEO and operating leaders, being in the business and in the decisions, tends to wait. We cover what that costs in why good CFOs leave good companies.

Which one the business needs, by stage

StageWhat the business needs mostType of CFO
Founder-led, finance done by a bookkeeperA design for what finance should becomeArchitect, often part-time or fractional
Growing past the first finance hireSystems, close, controls and the first teamBuilder
Established, scaling steadilyA function that runs, improves and develops peopleForeman
Preparing for a raise, sale or refinanceReporting and controls that survive outside scrutinyArchitect, then builder

Private equity adds a timetable. CFOs in the room described different profiles at different points in a hold period: a builder early, when reporting and controls have to be brought up to the sponsor's standard, a foreman through the middle years, and an architect again as exit preparation begins. Some sponsors value the CFO as a long-term partner across all of it. Others, as one participant put it, treat the seat as interchangeable and change the person as the stage changes.

How to cover the gap

Hire for the stage the business is entering, not the one it is leaving. Then decide how the other two roles get covered.

Architect missing. Bring in design input for a defined period: an experienced adviser, a board member with the relevant background, or a fractional CFO for the operating model and systems choices.

Builder missing. Add implementation capacity under the CFO rather than expecting them to do the build themselves. A controller, a systems lead or an outsourced team can carry the close and the implementation.

Foreman missing. Strengthen the layer below the CFO. A capable controller or finance manager who owns the workflow and the training lets an architect-type CFO stay in the business.

Name the gap in the hiring brief. A CFO hired as an architect and judged as a foreman will disappoint everyone, through no fault of their own.

What this will not tell you

It will not tell you who to hire. These labels describe what a business needs at a point in time, not a person's ceiling, and good CFOs move between them over a career.

It also says nothing about influence, which every one of the three needs and which none of them guarantees. We take that up in the CFO as reframer.

Frequently asked questions

What are the different types of CFO?

A useful split is architect, builder and foreman. The architect designs the finance function, the builder implements it, and the foreman runs and improves it. Most CFOs are strongest in one or two of the three.

Which type of CFO does a growing company need?

A company outgrowing its first finance hire usually needs a builder to put in systems, a reliable close and the first team. Before that, a part-time architect is often enough. Once the function is in place, a foreman keeps it running and improving.

Does a private equity-backed company need a different CFO at different stages?

Often, yes. Early in a hold period the priority is bringing reporting and controls up to the sponsor's standard, which suits a builder. The middle years reward a foreman, and exit preparation calls for architect work again.

Can one CFO be architect, builder and foreman?

Occasionally, but rarely at the same time. The roles need different distance from the detail and different amounts of time. It is usually better to hire for the stage ahead and cover the other roles with advisers or the team below the CFO.
