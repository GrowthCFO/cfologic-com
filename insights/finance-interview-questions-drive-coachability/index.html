Finance Interview Questions That Test for Drive and Coachability

By the CFOLogic team

Idea in brief

Six interview questions finance leaders use to test drive, curiosity and coachability in accounting and FP&A candidates, what a strong and a weak answer sound like, the red flags, and why these traits matter more now that AI handles the routine work junior staff used to learn on.

What to test for. Drive and coachability, alongside curiosity. Curiosity without drive produces interest; drive is what turns it into something the team can use.
Why it matters now. AI does more of the routine work that used to build judgment. The people who keep learning without being told to are the ones who will catch its mistakes.
How to ask. Ask for a specific, recent example and then ask what happened next. Generic answers to these questions are themselves the signal.
The red flag. A candidate who, asked about hard feedback, explains why the person giving it was wrong.

Most finance interview questions are good at one thing. A technical screen, a modelling test and a walk through the CV will tell you whether a candidate can do the job as it exists today. What they rarely tell you is whether the person will still be getting better in two years, when a share of the work they were hired for is done by software.

At CFO Alliance roundtables in Texas in early November 2026, that question came up in every room. Intellectual curiosity has long been near the top of the list of what CFOs hire for. The point several made this time was that curiosity on its own is not enough. It has to come with drive: the habit of learning something without being asked and putting it to work. The finance interview questions below are the ones those CFOs, FP&A leaders and recruiters said actually surface it.

Why drive and coachability matter more now

Junior finance staff used to build judgment through repetition. Reconciling accounts, tying out schedules and rebuilding a model line by line is tedious, and it is also how people learn what wrong looks like. AI now does much of that first pass.

That leaves a gap. Someone who never did the reps has less basis for knowing when an AI-generated answer is off. The people who close that gap are the ones who go looking for the understanding on their own, and who take correction well when they get it wrong. Those are the two traits worth testing for.

Six finance interview questions that surface them

1. "Tell me something you taught yourself and applied in your role that nobody asked you to do."

This is the clearest single test of drive. A strong answer names a specific skill or tool, explains why the candidate went after it, and describes what changed in their work as a result. A weak answer describes training the employer arranged, or a course with no application.

2. "What is the last thing you learned, and how did you put it to use?"

Close to the first, but it tests recency. People with real drive have a recent answer ready. Listen for how long ago it was and whether it reached the work.

3. "What is the last piece of tough feedback you received, and what did you do with it?"

This is the coachability test. A strong answer names the feedback plainly, admits what was fair in it, and describes a change in behaviour. The red flag, raised by more than one CFO in the room, is an answer that turns into an explanation of why the person giving the feedback was wrong.

4. "If your team gave you a finance nickname, what would it be and why?"

An FP&A leader uses this one. It sounds light, and it is useful because there is no rehearsed answer. It tells you how the candidate thinks they are seen, and whether that matches what their references say.

5. "Tell us something about you outside work that we would not learn from your CV."

A recruiter in the room makes a point of asking it. The answer often says more about work ethic and character than the work history does: what someone chose to pursue, and how far they took it.

6. "Tell us something you want us to know about you. Then tell us something you would rather we did not."

The second half is the useful part. Candidates who can name a real weakness without dressing it up are usually the ones who will tell you early when something is going wrong.

How to ask them well

Ask for one specific example, then ask a follow-up about what happened next. Most candidates can produce a general statement about loving to learn. Fewer can describe the spreadsheet they rebuilt, the query they wrote, or the feedback that changed how they run a close.

Use the same questions for every candidate for the role, and write down the answer, not your impression of it. It makes comparison fairer and stops the most fluent candidate from winning by default.

Score each answer on two things separately: did the person act without being told, and did the action change the work. A candidate can be curious and still score low on the second.

What these questions will not tell you

They will not tell you whether the person can do the technical work. Keep the technical screen. These questions sit alongside it, not in place of it.

They will not fix a team where nobody has time to teach. A driven junior hire in a team that never reviews their work or explains a correction will learn slowly, and may leave. If you hire for coachability, someone has to do the coaching. We look at the wider problem in the finance talent pipeline after AI.

Frequently asked questions

What interview questions reveal drive in a finance candidate?

Ask the candidate to describe something they taught themselves and applied at work without being asked, and what it changed. A specific, recent example with a visible result is the signal. A training course the employer arranged is not.

How do you test coachability in an interview?

Ask about the last piece of tough feedback the candidate received and what they did with it. A coachable candidate states the feedback plainly, says what was fair in it and describes a change. Explaining why the reviewer was wrong is a red flag.

Is intellectual curiosity enough when hiring for finance roles?

No. Curiosity needs to be paired with drive, the habit of learning without being asked and applying it. Curious people without drive tend to be interested in problems without moving them forward.

Why do drive and coachability matter more because of AI?

AI now does much of the routine work that junior finance staff used to learn from. People who seek out understanding on their own, and who accept correction, are better placed to catch AI errors and build judgment without those repetitions.

Should these questions replace technical interviews for accountants and FP&A analysts?

No. Keep the technical screen to test whether the candidate can do the work today. These questions test whether they will keep improving, and they work best alongside it.
